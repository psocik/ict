## Mobile Communications: A Blind Spot Security Leaders Can't Ignore

Mobile is now a core operational pillar for governments, critical infrastructure providers, and large enterprises. However, it is also a growing attack surface, as sensitive conversations increasingly happen across mobile-first channels where identity, location, and metadata move through global systems that are difficult to control and easy to underestimate. 🚨

BlackBerry's *State of Secure Communications 2026* research found that **83%** of security leaders report WhatsApp is used for sensitive discussions, and **75%** say employees bypass approved communications tools at least sometimes for efficiency. The channels where critical decisions happen are often subject to less governance and security review than the systems those organizations work so hard to protect. Many consumer messaging platforms were not designed for government-grade or critical-infrastructure communications. They were not built around the assurance requirements, identity controls, audit needs, or crisis demands that high-risk organizations face. Yet in practice, they are frequently used as if they can support those needs. This creates a significant gap between how these tools are used and what they were built to protect.

One of the most persistent misconceptions in mobile communications security is that end-to-end encryption is equivalent to a complete security model. End-to-end encryption is important; it helps protect message content in transit. But it does not, by itself, verify the identity of the people communicating. It does not secure a compromised device. It does not protect metadata, communication patterns, location information, or the broader context around a conversation. This distinction is notable because adversaries do not need to break encryption if they can exploit the systems, identities, and behaviors around it. An attacker may target account access through phishing, use impersonation or deepfakes to mislead staff, compromise a device to view messages after decryption, or collect metadata to map who is communicating, when, and how relationships inside an organization are structured. 🔍

For security leaders, the lesson is clear: encryption is one layer of protection, not the entire model. A secure communications approach must also account for identity verification, device integrity, metadata exposure, account controls, data retention, group membership, administrative visibility, and the workflows that determine how information moves. The mobile threat model needs to include the full communications stack. Security leaders should model telecom infrastructure, platform architecture, metadata, account access, identity verification, device integrity, storage, group membership, confidential and private conversation pathways, and crisis communications workflows. 📊

Adversaries can target communications infrastructure, account access, devices, or metadata that reveals patterns of activity. State-backed actors have also targeted consumer messaging accounts through phishing, impersonation, and account takeover. Organizations should identify where sensitive communications happen, determine which channels are approved versus tolerated, and ensure crisis plans include secure, verified alternatives before disruption occurs. Mobile communications are no longer a convenience layer; they are critical infrastructure, and adversaries already know it. ⚠️
