## Why We Need to Evolve Phishing Tests in the Age of AI

In cybersecurity, it is crucial to assess employees against current phishing techniques, especially as attackers leverage AI to craft more convincing and personalized messages. We must ask ourselves: are we preparing our teams for the phishing attacks they are likely to face today, or are we merely testing their ability to recognize outdated warning signs?

The traditional indicators of phishing that we trained employees to identify are becoming less reliable. Generative AI enables attackers to produce convincing messages, personalize lures, and enhance the quality of their communications. While not every phishing campaign utilizes AI, the presence of AI capabilities in 75% of the phishing kits analyzed on dark-web markets indicates a significant shift in the landscape. Furthermore, 82% of these kits offered deepfake features, underscoring the evolving threat.

Training programs need to adapt. If employees are primarily taught to look for spelling mistakes, generic greetings, or suspicious formatting, they may be ill-prepared for messages that use familiar names, relevant business contexts, and professional language. Training should emphasize verification in addition to recognition, encouraging employees to check unexpected requests through trusted channels, even when the message appears legitimate.

Phishing simulations often yield straightforward statistics: the percentage of recipients who clicked a link, entered credentials, or reported the message. While click rates may seem similar, the organization's ability to detect a real campaign varies. Reporting rates and the speed of reporting deserve more attention. Security teams should understand whether employees utilize the approved reporting channels and how quickly suspicious messages reach analysts. A test is a controlled exercise, not a guarantee of behavior during a real attack, which can depend on the recipient's state of mind.

The objective should be to identify weaknesses and enhance security, not to rank employees based on their performance. This approach should be reflected in how messages about failures are communicated.

Phishing simulations must evolve alongside the threats they aim to represent. Scenarios should mirror realistic business situations, including unexpected document sharing, supplier requests, and impersonation attempts. Employees should learn to verify unusual requests and report suspected attacks without fear of embarrassment or punishment.

As AI continues to transform how phishing messages are crafted and delivered, our security awareness must adapt. While yesterday's tests can provide valuable lessons, they should not dictate our readiness for tomorrow's challenges.
