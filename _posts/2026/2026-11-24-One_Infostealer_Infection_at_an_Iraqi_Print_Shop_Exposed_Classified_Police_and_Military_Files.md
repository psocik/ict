## One Infostealer Infection at an Iraqi Print Shop Exposed Classified Police and Military Files

In September 2023, an infostealer infection hit a small print shop in Qayyarah, Iraq - a walk-in vendor that, it turns out, at least four Iraqi police units and an army brigade all paid to type up their paperwork. Neither the police nor the army was breached. But their documents were on that machine, including a 189-name list that labels each person "the ISIS member" beside his village, his job, and whether he still owns his house. The file that started this investigation was a spreadsheet-style Word document, recovered from an infostealer log dated 30 September 2023. Its title, in the compiling institution's own words, was an inventory of ISIS-linked houses within the responsibility sector of the Imam Gharbi Police Station - and it ran to 189 named individuals across eight villages, one row per person, each labelled in the name column as اسم الداعشي: "the ISIS member's name."  

A police station's internal list of alleged ISIS affiliates should be on a police computer. This one was in a folder with more than a hundred other Word files - and the further into that folder we went, the less it looked like anything a police station owned. Directly exposed - their own internal casework was sitting on the machine: Imam Gharbi Police Station - the 189-name ISIS house inventory. Al-Hurriya police station - a 2023 detainee register, a different station entirely. Qayyarah Police Section, Nineveh Police Directorate - a "basic register" cover sheet dated 2022, and a charge slip booking a named defendant under Article 456 of the Iraqi Penal Code, case year 2023. The Ministry of Interior's Community Policing Division for Nineveh - a blank visitor's log on its letterhead. The Iraqi Army's 76th Infantry Brigade (Movements Section) - a daily duty roster and two classification-marked message forms. That is at least four separate police entities and an army brigade - bodies that do not share filing systems, buildings, or chains of command. No officer has legitimate custody of all of that. No single unit's clerk produces all of it. At that point the "government machine" assumption stopped holding together.  

Additionally, a second layer of exposure involved institutions and officials named as recipients inside petitions the same shop typed for ordinary residents. These included The Prime Minister of Iraq's office, as Commander-in-Chief of the Armed Forces - two separate letters, each from a different retired officer, The Qayyarah Investigative Court, The Popular Mobilization Forces' administrative and financial affairs department, The Ministry of Electricity, The Nineveh Health Directorate, The Husseini Shrine's General Secretariat in Karbala, and The Qayyarah national-ID office. The log gave us nothing in the other direction either. An institutional machine is usually easy to identify from an infostealer log: a domain-joined profile, a work address in the autofill data, saved credentials for a government portal, an organisation name on the Windows account. This machine had none of it.  

Key details from the infostealer log include:  
- **Date of capture:** 30 September 2023  
- **Location:** Ninawa province, Iraq  
- **Windows identity:** PC - no institution or individual named  
- **Domain membership:** None detected  
- **Government credentials:** None - no ministry, police, or military accounts saved  
- **Material recovered:** 100+ Word documents, one desktop folder.  

The software fleet itself pointed to a print counter's machine, not an office desk's, featuring driver suites for three competing printer/scanner brands at once - Canon, Epson, and three separate HP Enterprise-class multifunction devices - the full Adobe design suite, three OCR packages, and Kelk, a professional Arabic typesetting program built for exactly one trade: laying out documents for print.
