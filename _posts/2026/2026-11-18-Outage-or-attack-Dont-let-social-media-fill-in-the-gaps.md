## Outage or Attack? Don't Let Social Media Fill in the Gaps

The problem with reporting on computer failures is how quickly "the system is down" becomes "the system is under attack." Non-malicious causes include faulty software updates, configuration mistakes, equipment failures, power interruptions, and damaged cables. Cyberattacks can also disrupt services by overwhelming them with traffic or using ransomware to make systems unusable. From the outside, both can have you staring at much the same unhelpful error message. US Secretary of State Marco Rubio felt the need to dismiss widespread concerns that the recent tech outages in the past week are connected.

During an outage, users want immediate answers, but investigators need time to establish what's gone wrong. In that information vacuum, speculation thrives. Some recent events include:
- **European refrigeration attacks** disrupted cooling systems at some businesses.
- **Monitoring systems** at three French blood-storage sites were affected, but staff intervened before any blood products or samples were compromised.
- **Microsoft 365 outage** was caused by a latent software defect in networking gateways, leading several organizations to lose a full day of work.
- **FAA nationwide ground stop** in the US due to a telecommunications failure disrupted some air traffic control communications, resulting in thousands of flight cancellations and ongoing delays.
- **JPMorgan Chase** faced banking problems caused by a software bug in an on-premises hardware system that slowed transaction processing, delaying transactions without lasting damage.
- **Optimizely Web Experimentation** supply-chain attack served malicious code on 178 popular websites, which was fixed rapidly, but visitors may have been exposed to malicious code that infected their devices.

There is an important distinction between outages caused by bugs and those caused by attacks, but the cause alone doesn't tell us how disruptive an incident will be. A technical failure can cause enormous problems without an attacker being involved. And an attack can cause harm without producing a spectacular outage. The 90-day blackout claim shows how quickly genuine events can be turned into evidence for a much bigger story. Social media users turned a routine US emergency alert test into a prediction of a 90-day blackout. Some posts pointed to the Microsoft outage and FAA ground stop as evidence that the disaster was already unfolding. In reality, the alert was a scheduled test, and the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) explicitly said there were "no blackouts, no secret emergencies."

AI is helping both software vendors and attackers find more bugs. That can mean larger updates from Microsoft, Google, and others, covering more vulnerabilities at a faster pace. AI can also help attackers find ways to exploit those vulnerabilities, putting pressure on security teams and consumers to install those patches as soon as possible. Companies need to explain what they know, what they're still investigating, and what customers should do:
- **Keep your software updated.**
- **Protect your devices.**
- **Have a backup plan** for services you depend on.

But don't let someone else's take on things fill the gaps in what is known.
