## China Cyber Ai

The PRC's cyber campaigns are widely interpreted as an ecosystem that the government in Beijing no longer fully directs. A senior FBI official has publicly stated that the Chinese "hacker-for-hire ecosystem has gotten out of control", and indictments have labeled it reckless and indiscriminate. However, the evidence suggests a different narrative. The central government has not lost control of its cyber campaigns; rather, it tolerates the looseness they produce as the price of operating at scale. This is significant for affected states: a government that tolerates such looseness is likely to change course only when the cost of maintaining it outweighs the benefits.

Over more than a decade, the PRC has established the laws, institutions, force structure, and talent base necessary to run multiple cyber campaigns in parallel. It has also fostered a commercial market in access, infrastructure, and tooling that has largely developed independently. Reporting duties require newly discovered vulnerabilities to be sent to the state, which stockpiles them for selective use and also purchases exploits for large-scale operations. Dutch military intelligence assesses that the PRC's offensive cyber capabilities are now likely on par with those of the United States.

Control is bounded, not lost. The central government allows its operators to work loosely, enforcing a lower limit while maintaining an upper limit that has yet to be tested. Between these two boundaries, contractors compromise targets on their own initiative and sell the results to state buyers. Espionage blends with financially motivated crime, and tools and exploits proliferate after use. The central government has not corrected any of this. Since 2021, over twenty enforcement actions have suspended leading firms from military procurement or excluded them from it, all concerning how the firms won contracts rather than how they operate. Correction of operator behavior occurred only once, around the 2015 US-China agreement, after sustained pressure on the overall relationship. However, this effect did not last. A state that can correct its actions but chooses not to has made a deliberate choice. Responsibility does not depend on tasking; a state that builds up the system, declines to correct its operations, and continues to purchase what it produces is responsible for that output, regardless of whether it ordered each intrusion.

The upper limit—the ceiling—has not been tested. No PRC campaign has been shown to aim at disrupting the operation of foreign critical infrastructure, although access to power, water, and transport systems has been maintained for years. Access that PRC actors hold in critical infrastructure should be treated as access they may one day use to disrupt it. The number of PRC cyber campaigns is likely to increase as AI and a competitive supplier market make operations cheaper. Responses primarily address single incidents rather than the patterns they form and rely mainly on technical instruments. The economic and diplomatic channels to which the PRC has visibly reacted in the past remain largely unused for cyber conduct.

### Seven Measures for Affected States
None of these measures require new legal powers. However, two things are still missing: a shared record and a pre-agreed response. Default attribution comes first, with the others supporting it: hold the PRC responsible by default for its share of any campaign with a China nexus, without first proving that it tasked the specific intrusion. Supply-layer disruption targets infrastructure that many operators share and produces evidence under judicial authority. Collective cost imposition utilizes the accumulated record for economic and diplomatic measures. Willing states must act together and agree in advance to share the costs of Chinese counter-pressure. Ceiling contingency prepares for the disruption of critical infrastructure: continuity planning for an adversary already inside, a coalition response agreed in advance, and a public statement that even holding such access will draw consequences. The PRC has built up the system, tolerates its operation, and continues to purchase what it produces. The delegation is the policy, and affected states are entitled to respond accordingly.
